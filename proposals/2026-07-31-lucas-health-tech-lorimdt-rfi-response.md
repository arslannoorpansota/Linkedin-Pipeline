**11. Platform architecture.**
Cloud-native and container-based, deployed to your own US AWS environment as a managed, HIPAA-eligible footprint, which is the "on-premises to the customer's cloud" model your FAQ Q46 describes. Multi-region (East and West) for high availability and disaster recovery, with a day-one availability target around 98% maturing toward the five-nines objective by general availability (your FAQ Q46). Proposed stack: containerized services on ECS or EKS, PostgreSQL for the longitudinal patient-journey data model, a FHIR integration layer, and infrastructure-as-code for reproducible per-site connection configuration and tenant isolation.

**12. FHIR R4 support.**
We build native HL7 FHIR R4 support. For LORiMDT specifically:
- Bidirectional at Tier 1 on the Epic connection: broad reads (Patient, Condition, MedicationRequest, Observation, DiagnosticReport, Procedure, and notes) and a narrow, governed write for the MDT note (see Section 2.3 and your FAQ Q28 and Q36).
- SMART on FHIR with EHR launch as the target Epic access pattern, distributed through App Orchard, so LORiMDT opens in context with patient and encounter established. A standalone backend and API mode is architected as the portable fallback and for non-Epic sites (your FAQ Q27).
- US Core profiles as the interoperability baseline, and mCODE as the target oncology profile for the cancer data model, extended with LORiMDT's governance elements (the clinical handshake, BCLC-CUSE mapping, liver-function gating, and the re-entry loop) where mCODE does not reach (your FAQ Q24 and Q38).
- A three-tier capture model as the design premise, because not all 850+ elements are FHIR-populable (your FAQ Q34): Auto-EHR from FHIR where the resource exists, AI-assisted drafting for clinician confirmation, and clinician-attested entry where no discrete source exists, with an HL7 v2 fallback at non-Epic sites.

**13. EHR integration.**
Epic and Oracle Health / Cerner via FHIR R4, with an HL7 v2 fallback where a resource is not exposed (your FAQ Q32). We design for separate, independent Epic installs across sites, which is the harder and more general case, with per-site connection configuration and tenant isolation, so anything that works across independent installs also works if two sites share a Community Connect instance (your FAQ Q29). Integration methods: FHIR REST APIs as the primary path, SMART on FHIR launch, HL7 v2 as the fallback, and CDA where needed.

On App Orchard specifically: we are not currently a listed Epic App Orchard developer. Our existing healthcare platform integrates with legacy and standards-based systems rather than Epic. We would complete Epic App Orchard registration and the certification required for the encounter-scoped MDT-note write as part of the Tier 1 build, and we budget for it. Your FAQ Q16 already frames the pilot as first-time integration bring-up at the sites, so this is planned work, not a surprise.

**14. Workflow configuration.**
We deliver a configurable clinical workflow engine, delivered configured to LORiMDT's defined pilot workflows, with your clinical-governance roles able to adjust configuration afterward. Not hardcoded screens, and not a bare toolkit you configure alone (your FAQ Q44). The core pilot workflow is the case lifecycle: initiation and data population, asynchronous specialist pre-review, synchronous MDT deliberation, recommendation and treatment-plan capture, the clinical handshake (ownership assignment plus acknowledgment), note generation and write-back, and the iterative reassessment and re-entry loop. It is conditional and includes stop points: required-field enforcement gates submission, per-cancer-type conditional fields drive visibility, and the Level 1 soft-stop halts progression until the accountable clinician acknowledges. Access is role- and site-scoped (submitting clinician, MDT chair, specialist reviewer, coordinator).

**15. Rules engine and trigger logic.**
We build a configurable, versioned rules engine, not hardcoded logic. It supports triggers on lab values, imaging and LI-RADS results, staging criteria, and time-based escalations. Rule changes are governed, versioned, and audit-logged (who changed what, when, and why), managed by your authorized clinical-governance roles (your FAQ Q26). Cases in flight are pinned to the rule version under which they were being evaluated, so a mid-stream change does not retroactively rewrite a decision the board already acted on. Where a change is clinically material, the platform flags affected in-flight cases for re-review rather than mutating them. On ownership (your FAQ Q25): we build the engine, Lucas Health Tech owns the rule definitions and governance framework, and the clinical thresholds are guideline-anchored (AASLD, NCCN, ESMO, BCLC) and specialist-validated, not vendor-authored.

**16. AI/ML capabilities.**
This is our deepest strength. Our CTO, Arslan, spent the last year working with OpenAI on training their video-generation models, so the AI side is real rather than a wrapper, and our team ships production LLM and RAG systems. For LORiMDT, AI is applied narrowly, with a clinician always in control:
- Automated note generation at Tier 1. The platform assembles a structured MDT note from the case's own structured data plus the board's decision and drafts it into a formatted note. This is AI-assisted drafting, not autonomous authorship. The accountable clinician reviews it, edits the narrative and the discrete recommendation and plan elements, and only then commits it through the handshake (your FAQ Q33 and Q35).
- AI-assisted capture at Tier 1: drafting field values from available data for clinician confirmation, inside the three-tier model.
- Later tiers: NLP extraction from narrative radiology reports (Tier 3, not a Tier 1 promise, per your FAQ Q30), treatment-sequencing suggestions, trial-eligibility screening, and guideline-concordance checking with deviation flags.

We do not put AI in the decision path. It reduces transcription and drafting burden. The clinician owns every committed record.

**17. API and extensibility.**
REST APIs across platform services, webhook and event support, and an internal orchestration engine that can optionally consume FHIR Subscriptions where a site exposes them (for example, to refresh data when new results post) but does not require them. Tier 1 orchestration is user-action-driven through the platform's own state machine (your FAQ Q37). Third-party integrations such as Open Evidence at Tier 3 are designed as pluggable capabilities.

## 2.3 Clinical workflow and governance

**18. MDT / tumor board support.**
Yes. The case is modeled as a persistent, versioned, collaboratively edited object with explicit state, not a document that exists only during a live meeting (your FAQ Q22). We support the hybrid board: asynchronous preparation (case assembly, data population, specialist pre-review over days) followed by a synchronous sign-off event where the recommendation is deliberated and the clinical handshake (ownership assignment plus acknowledgment) is executed. Fully asynchronous virtual boards are supported and confirmed per site during discovery. The full audit trail of decisions, recommendations, and acknowledgments is captured (see Q21).

**19. Role-based views.**
Yes. Role- and site-scoped dashboards for the submitting clinician, MDT chair, specialist reviewers (hepatology, transplant and surgical, medical and radiation oncology, radiology, pathology), and the coordinator. Users see the steps and cases appropriate to their role and tenant (your FAQ Q44). Chair, specialist, and admin dashboard depth expands at Tier 2.

**20. Patient journey tracking.**
Yes. The core of LORiMDT is a longitudinal patient-journey data model that persists clinical decisions, clinical reasoning, and trigger state across the full arc of care, rather than point-in-time snapshots (your FAQ Q12). Source data stays in the EHR and LIS and is ingested via FHIR R4; LORiMDT maintains the persistent governance layer on top. A case is submitted, becomes read-only, is routed for review, produces a treatment plan, and re-opens through iterative clinical reassessment when conditions change.

**21. Documentation and audit trail.**
We build to the Tier 1 audit floor you set in your FAQ Q45: authentication events; PHI access (views and searches); case create, update, and delete with field-level change history; case state transitions; the clinical handshake events (ownership assignment and acknowledgment) with actor, timestamp, and captured intent; note generation and EHR write-back; escalation and soft-stop triggers and their resolution; configuration and permission changes; and integration transactions (FHIR pull and push) with success or failure. Entries are attributable via SSO identity, tamper-evident, and immutably timestamped. On the handshake (your FAQ Q23): for Tier 1 an attributable, timestamped, audit-logged acknowledgment is sufficient, and full 21 CFR Part 11 is not a Tier 1 requirement, but we architect the signature and audit layer to be Part 11-upgradeable without re-architecture for your trial-enabling roadmap.

## 2.4 Security and compliance

**22. HIPAA compliance.**
The platform runs in a US-based, HIPAA-eligible AWS environment with US data residency. We execute BAAs. PHI is encrypted, access is least-privilege and client-governed, and the audit trail (Q21) covers PHI access. Production PHI is processed only in the US environment.

**23. Certifications.**
We will be direct: ElectroCom does not currently hold SOC 2 Type II, HITRUST, or ISO 27001. What we do is build to the controls those frameworks specify (encryption, least-privilege access, audit logging, change management, and BAAs), and we will commit contractually to achieving SOC 2 Type II within 12 months of contract signing, with ISO 27001 for our delivery operations on a similar horizon. If a certification is a hard gate for you at pilot, tell us and we will scope the timeline and cost into the proposal directly rather than around it.

**24. Data encryption.**
Encryption at rest (AES-256, KMS-managed keys) and in transit (TLS 1.2 or higher). Field-level handling for the most sensitive PHI where warranted, with key rotation and access logging on the key store.

**25. Access controls.**
RBAC scoped by tenant and clinical role. Authentication via SSO and SMART on FHIR against each site's existing identity provider: SMART on FHIR for Epic sites, and SAML or OIDC federation to the site's enterprise IdP (Azure AD / Entra, Okta, or PingFederate) more generally. We maintain no separate password store (your FAQ Q41 and Q48). MFA is enforced at the IdP, with session management and re-authentication for sensitive actions. For patients, a stable internal patient identifier resolves site-specific MRNs via MPI-style matching, with human adjudication for uncertain matches, because in oncology a false merge is a patient-safety event (your FAQ Q31 and Q41).

**26. Data residency.**
Production PHI resides in US AWS regions. The application, its database, and its authentication endpoints deploy to your US-region AWS account, enforced by region restriction, network controls, and IAM policy (your FAQ Q48).

Disclosure, per your FAQ Q57 and Q59: ElectroCom's engineering team works from our Multan, Pakistan office, and our US entity and engagement accountability sit at our Celina, Texas headquarters. We state that plainly rather than implying a US-resident engineering bench we do not have.

The control that matters is not where engineers sit but who can reach production PHI:
- No standing human access to production. Deployment is via CI/CD from version control, and engineers hold no interactive production credentials.
- Development and testing run entirely against de-identified or synthetic data, never production PHI. That is the split your FAQ Q59 describes.
- Break-glass access is time-boxed, individually attributed, Lucas Health Tech-approved, fully session-logged, and reachable only through a jump path you control.
- Logs are PHI-scrubbed, so routine debugging never exposes patient data.

If you require that every individual capable of break-glass access be US-resident, we will staff that role US-side and price it. Tell us and we will scope it directly rather than around it.

## Appendix B. Case study: legacy clinical platform modernization (client anonymized)

**Engagement.** Modernization of a legacy desktop clinical application into a production cloud web platform, delivered by ElectroCom's engineering team.

**Starting point.** The client ran a desktop application that had served hospital clinical and diagnostic-lab workflows for over a decade. It was stable but aging: the deployment model blocked multi-location and remote access, the interface had fallen behind current clinical-software expectations, and the architecture could not support the client's expansion plans.

**What we built.** Over roughly 20 months we rebuilt the platform module by module into a modern React and TypeScript cloud application, while the existing system stayed live for hospital users. We preserved the backend and clinical-data integrity while replacing the frontend architecture, introduced a design system for long-term maintainability, and validated each module in real operational use before moving to the next. Modules delivered included quality control, patient records, clinical reporting and diagnostic-test workflows, appointment scheduling and templates, and staff rostering.

**Result.** A cloud-based, vendor-neutral clinical platform now live across hospitals in Australia and the UK, on an architecture built for cross-market expansion. Delivery was incremental and never took the clinical service offline.

**Relevance to LORiMDT.** The same terrain: real clinical data, multi-hospital operation, modernization under live operational constraints, and a data model built to be vendor-neutral and standards-ready. The stack (React, TypeScript, containerized services, cloud deployment) is the one we propose for LORiMDT.

Full detail, including the client, is available at the demo stage.
